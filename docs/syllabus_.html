Description

Welcome to PUBH 523/623! This course introduces programming in R for public health, medicine, and related fields. We’ll focus on reproducible workflows, data manipulation, visualization, and reporting: working in project-based folders, transforming and summarizing data, making plots, generating dynamic reports, and building summary tables. No prior statistics or coding experience is expected. By the end, you’ll be comfortable starting a data exploration project in R and ready to use these skills in future analysis courses.

Course Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, you should be able to…

  1. Set up and manage project-based folders to build reproducible workflows.
  2. Clean, filter, restructure, and summarize datasets using standard R toolkits.
  3. Create clear, publication-quality data visualizations that represent trends and summaries.
  4. Launch a data exploration project on a new dataset, preparing it for advanced statistical analysis.

How each activity is graded

Practice (40%)

Everyone has a different amount of time for coursework, and practice is for learning, so grading is simple: you get a check if you complete at least 75% of the question parts, whether your answers are right or wrong. Each of the 9 practice assignments counts equally.

  • On time: you get the check and feedback on one or more problems.
  • Late: no grade penalty. You still get the check if submitted before the course ends, just no feedback (unless you email me to ask). Compare your work to the solutions or bring it to office hours.

Practice is the most helpful work in this class, so I encourage you to keep up with it and put in what effort you can!

Office hours visit (2%)

Come to office hours at least once during the quarter for full credit. You don’t need a question! Stopping by to say hi, working quietly on practice, or listening to others’ questions all count. If none of the office hour times work for you, email me and we’ll find another time to meet.

Project Labs (40%)

There are 4 labs, each worth an equal share of the 40%. Labs walk you through the coding and deliverables that build toward your poster. Each lab is graded with a rubric that focuses on completing the required code and deliverables and clear, reproducible work (e.g., code that runs and outputs that match what’s asked). You’ll get feedback on each lab, and I encourage you to use it in your next lab and your poster.

Labs follow the late policy below: one no-questions-asked 3-day extension, then 1 point off per day late.

Project Poster (18%)

The poster (an information sheet) is your chance to bring together what you built in the labs and decide what’s most important to share about your data. It is graded with a rubric that looks at accurate, reproducible analysis, clear and well-designed visualizations and tables, and communicating your findings to a public health audience. Because it’s due at the end of the quarter, feedback will be lighter, but feel free to email me with questions about your grade. And you can add this project to your resume!

If I reach out during an emergency, it’s only because I’m worried, and you do NOT need to respond until you’re able.

Regrade Policy

If you think something was graded incorrectly, first compare your answer to the solutions. If you still think a regrade is warranted, email me the question and a short explanation within one week of the assignment being returned.

Generative AI Policy

This policy is adapted from the OHSU-PSU SPH Biostatistics program’s AI policy, which classifies PUBH 523/623 as a foundational course. Everything you need for this class is below.

Using AI is not required in this class. If you choose to use it, please follow these guidelines. AI tools (like ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini, or an AI assistant built into RStudio) are becoming an essential skill for public health data work, so the goal is to use them responsibly, not avoid them.

Important

Think of this class as assembling a toolbox. A new handyperson might need help finding their tools or remembering which one does what, but eventually using them becomes second nature.

For now, generative AI can help us find and use our tools, but we’re working toward using them as second nature!

Four guiding principles

  1. Learning comes first. Ask yourself: Is AI helping me learn this skill, or doing the skill for me? If it’s doing the skill the assignment is meant to teach, don’t use it that way.
  2. Understanding is the priority. You should be able to explain and reproduce (without AI) any code or conclusion you turn in. Following along with an AI answer is not the same as understanding it!
  3. Build the foundation first. In this course, writing and debugging R code from a blank script is the skill we’re practicing. That’s why some AI uses are limited here, not because AI is untrustworthy.
  4. Be honest about AI use. Tell me when and how you used AI (see the AI Use Statement below).

What’s allowed

There may be exceptions. For example, if a question asks you to explain what a function does, you can’t paste in AI’s explanation. When in doubt, just ask me!

Never allowed

  • Copying and pasting AI-generated answers into your assignment
  • Pasting all or part of an assignment into AI and asking for the solution
  • Presenting AI output as your own reasoning
  • Uploading confidential or restricted data (including any data with protected health information) into an AI tool, unless it’s a university-approved platform under a data use agreement
  • Using AI-generated datasets or output as if they came from the assigned data and methods
  • Using AI-generated references, results, or facts without verifying them yourself
  • Submitting AI-assisted work without an AI Use Statement

Even for allowed uses, always inspect AI-generated code and its output, whether it’s a whole chunk or a single comma. You’re responsible for making sure it does what it’s supposed to.

Practice is graded on completion, so I’d much rather see a half-written answer that shows your thinking than a polished answer from AI. And if AI helped you understand something, tell me! I’d love to incorporate it into future classes.

AI Use Statement

If you used AI on an assignment, add a brief statement at the top that includes which tool(s) you used, what for (a sentence or two), and where it contributed to any written interpretations. For example:

AI Use Statement: Claude was used to explain an error message in Q3 and to give grammar feedback on my poster summary. All code review, reasoning, and conclusions are my own.

You don’t need to share a link or transcript of your conversation.

If the guidelines aren’t followed

If I notice AI was used outside these guidelines, I’ll reach out to let you know which assignment and which guideline, and you can choose to:

  1. Take a 0 on the assignment as submitted,
  2. Redo the assignment without AI for a new score, or
  3. Explain to me (by email or in a meeting) exactly how you used AI and show that you can do the work on your own. If that works out, you keep your original grade; if not, the question(s) where AI was used get 0 points.

First-time, good-faith mistakes (like forgetting an AI Use Statement) will generally be treated as a learning moment, not an integrity issue.

Course Expectations

  • Commitment to your success. I believe everyone can succeed in this course, and I’ve designed it to support your learning. Reach out anytime about anything that’s unclear.
